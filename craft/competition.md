# Prior art, competition and positioning

Dunford's [positioning method](https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning) starts with what a customer would do without the offering, then connects distinctive capabilities to value, best-fit audience and category. Its examples are mainly B2B; for games, genre expectations and an emotional payoff often carry the equivalent job.

Search the user's words for the task, genre and workaround, not just the project name. Include a direct competing product, a substitute, an open-source/free option where relevant, and the current process or doing nothing. Search official sites, repositories, store listings, changelogs and firsthand demonstrations. Date claims about pricing, availability and features. Review/comment aggregations describe their contributors, not all customers.

Use competitor records for verified dimensions with a source URL and checked date. If a feature is not documented, write “not established from this source”; absence is not proof that it does not exist. Keep claims separate from your interpretation. Avoid declaring a project unique after a few searches. Name the search terms, sources, geographic/language coverage and remaining uncertainty.

Deliver a compact matrix of audience, promised outcome, distinctive approach, price/access, proof and friction. Then state an original positioning hypothesis: intended person → situation → useful outcome → credible distinction → proof. “Made with AI” explains development; it becomes customer value only if the audience values the resulting benefit. Be truthful about material AI behavior. Do not infer legal freedom to operate from marketing prior-art research.
